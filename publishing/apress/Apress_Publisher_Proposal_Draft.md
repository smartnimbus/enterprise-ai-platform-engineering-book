## Part 1. Proposal strategy: the acquisition narrative

### 1.1 One-sentence proposition

**Building an AI agent is an application problem; operating many agents safely is a platform engineering problem.**

### 1.2 The proposal story, in acquisition order

**The adoption transition.** Organisations can build or purchase individual AI agents with growing ease. The harder question emerges when several business domains need the same identity, tool access, security boundaries, model routing, evidence collection and production support. An isolated successful pilot does not establish a sustainable shared service.

**The consequential decisions.** Leaders must decide whether a shared Fabric is warranted at all; where to build, buy, embed or partner; how to allocate accountability; who can authorise an agent's external actions; what happens when context conflicts; which evidence permits autonomy increases; what a successful task costs; and how a use case or the entire Fabric can be retired.

**The missing reader product.** Programming-focused books explain agent construction. Governance titles explain controls. Platform engineering books explain shared-service ownership. This book is organised around the *decisions connecting those disciplines*, and supplies worked artefacts that carry those choices into operation.

**The method.** Four Structural Layers and eight Control Planes give the reader a consistent reference model. The 30-page Decision Atlas applies the same one-page contract to every consequential choice: context, default, trade-off, deviation conditions, reversibility, and an artefact. The lifecycle stages and playbooks show how to turn the decision into action and evidence.

**The proof.** Meridian Financial is an explicitly fictional enterprise example. It demonstrates choices, interruptions, failures, escalation, operating consequences and retirement. Technical examples and evidence specimens are labelled as illustrative unless independently verified. The primary samples, Chapters 16 and 13, are designed to demonstrate non-bypassable authorisation and provenance-aware context handling.

**The buyer value.** An enterprise architect can defend choices. An AI platform lead can assign service boundaries. A security architect can inspect enforcement evidence. A platform engineering lead can onboard more than one use case. An SRE manager can identify the responsibility for failure, recovery and retirement.

### 1.3 Positioning and commercial differentiation

**Category:** Enterprise AI platform engineering and agentic-system reference architecture. **Commercial format:** Decision-led professional reference, not an agent-programming tutorial, vendor manual, compliance handbook or certification guide. **Differentiating unit:** A completed decision page linked to its control owner and operating artefact. **Boundaries:** The book does not claim to invent agent fabrics, gateways, control planes, autonomy governance or delegation protocols. Its contribution is their consistent application across an enterprise decision and operating lifecycle.

### 1.4 Acquisition statement for an editor

Apress has published and commissioned books on enterprise agent adoption, AI governance, autonomous identity and platform engineering. The proposed manuscript complements that catalogue by making architecture decisions and their operational evidence the organising principle. Its commercial promise is a vendor-neutral, cross-use-case reference a professional can reopen at an investment choice, design review, release gate, incident or retirement decision.

## Part 3. Executive summary — proposal-ready

### Executive summary

**Building an AI agent is an application problem; operating many agents safely is a platform engineering problem.**

As organisations adopt AI agents across business functions, successful isolated applications create a second engineering problem. Teams need consistent rules for identity, delegated authority, tool access, tenant isolation, model selection, context provenance, verification, operational support and cost attribution. Some organisations should share these controls. Others should retain domain-local delivery or buy capabilities inside existing products. The difficult work is deciding when each approach is justified and making the resulting choice accountable.

*The Enterprise AI Agent Fabric: Designing, Governing and Operating AI Agents at Scale* is a vendor-neutral professional reference for those decisions. The primary readers are enterprise architects and AI platform leads responsible for multiple agentic use cases. Security architects, platform engineering leads and SRE managers will use it to review authorisation boundaries, shared-service contracts, reliability and incidents. The book assumes readers already understand common enterprise software architecture concepts; it does not teach a specific agent framework.

The manuscript provides three complementary resources. First, a **30-page Decision Atlas** gives every major design choice a standard one-page contract: context, default, trade-off, deviation conditions, reversibility and an explicit decision artefact. Choices include whether to adopt a shared Fabric, build versus buy, tenant isolation, delegated authority, mutable-action enforcement, model routing, autonomy, evaluation, economics and retirement. Second, a **canonical reference architecture** separates four Structural Layers—Execution Environment, Tooling, Context and Lifecycle—from eight Control Planes—Identity, Gateway, Observability, Verification, Governance, FinOps, Data Protection and Interoperability. Third, an eleven-stage lifecycle and ten operational playbooks connect the architecture to funding, onboarding, release, incident response, scaling and withdrawal.

Unlike a framework-specific build guide, the book teaches readers how to defend a system choice and verify its operating consequences. The examples move from an architecture decision record to a named control owner, an enforcement point, a verification condition and an evidence record. A gateway chapter demonstrates how to block an unauthorised mutable action and prove that bypass routes are closed. A context chapter demonstrates how retrieved material and memory carry provenance, how conflicts are recognised, and when the system must escalate instead of inventing a resolution. These two chapters are the proposed acquisition samples because they show the decision method at technical depth.

A single fictional organisation, Meridian Financial, supplies a restrained running case. Its teams confront duplicated integrations, competing authority, context conflict, security incidents, operational cost, expansion and retirement. Each scene introduces an architectural decision or operating artefact; it does not substitute fiction for technical evidence. Synthetic figures and records will be labelled as such, and external technical claims will be cited and verified.

The book is designed for reference use after first reading. A reader preparing an investment appraisal can open the sourcing or No-Fabric decision. A reviewer assessing a release can consult behavioural assertions, policy enforcement receipts and the relevant playbook. An operator investigating a failed action can identify the responsible boundaries and reconstruct the required evidence. A leader terminating a service can follow the retirement requirements instead of treating deployment as the endpoint of design.

The planned work comprises 58 chapters in eight parts, approximately 478 pages including front matter and index, 30 one-page decisions and ten playbooks. It belongs in the Apress professional technology catalogue alongside enterprise agent adoption, governance, architecture and platform engineering books, while distinguishing itself through a single decision-and-evidence method applied from strategic intent through retirement. It makes no claim to own industry terms such as *agent fabric* or *autonomy envelope*. The intended value is an integrated reference, consistent decision contracts and complete worked artefacts.

The proposed author will supply independently reviewed technical samples, an annotated contents list, a complete decision-page sampler, technical figures and a provenance and permissions register as part of the commissioning package. The production plan assumes parallel authoring, technical review, editorial review and illustration; the full schedule will be confirmed against actual resource commitments before contract execution.

## Part 4. Market need — proposal-ready

### The reader problem

Enterprise agent adoption changes the unit of responsibility. A team can evaluate a single assistant by the quality of its responses or the completion of a bounded workflow. Once several domains deploy agents that call external tools, mutate business records, retrieve protected material and depend on shared model services, the organisation also has to govern the boundaries between those use cases. This introduces investment, identity, isolation, operational and financial decisions that cannot be settled by selecting a model or agent framework.

The book is aimed at organisations in this transition. The reader is not necessarily building an entire Fabric. The first important decision may be that a shared Fabric is unjustified. Where several funded use cases require common control contracts, the same reader needs to allocate responsibilities across domain teams, platform teams, security, SRE and FinOps, without centralising ownership of every business outcome.

### Evidence of a growing professional need

The existing market research establishes three directional signals. First, the 2025 McKinsey AI survey reported substantial experimentation with agents but a smaller share of respondents scaling them in production business functions. Second, its August 2026 follow-up identified a higher share of scaled adoption among large organisations and recorded operating cost as a constraint for a material portion of respondents. Third, 2025 DORA research drew attention to internal platform quality and organisational conditions as determinants of AI adoption outcomes. These findings support *relevance*, not a forecast of sales or a claim that all enterprises require a shared Fabric.

**Evidence treatment before submission:** Cite and date each survey in the publisher-facing reference list; distinguish survey respondents from all businesses and scaling in *one function* from scaling a common enterprise platform. Do not convert agent adoption statistics into a direct estimate of the book's market size. [Attach the verified 2025 and 2026 study references from the existing commercial research dossier.] 

### Why platform engineering is the organising discipline

Shared agent services inherit familiar platform problems—service ownership, multi-tenancy, interfaces, reliability, cost allocation and customer onboarding—while adding autonomy-specific controls. A tool call may carry a delegated human identity. An apparently helpful retrieval may cross a data boundary. A model change may alter the sequence of tool choices without changing the application API. An agent may resume queued work after authority has expired. These are reasons to build operational contracts, not proof that every organisation should build a central platform.

The manuscript teaches the choice between shared and local solutions, and then follows the consequences. It links a control definition to its designated owner, enforcement mechanism, test, exception path and operating evidence. This format is useful where enterprises must defend the design to security, audit, finance and product leadership, while still giving domain teams a workable onboarding route.

### Why governance and operating model belong together

A governance policy that cannot stop an external action is incomplete. A release process that measures only model quality cannot establish whether the agent acts within its authority. A platform operating model that cannot allocate incident ownership or charge back cost cannot scale responsibly. By connecting these concerns through decision records and playbooks, the book gives readers a way to move from recommendations to operational tests and artefacts.

### Market scope and limits

Primary readers are practitioners making enterprise-level architecture and platform decisions. The book is not positioned for introductory AI users, individual prompt engineers, buyers seeking vendor rankings, or developers wanting a single cloud implementation tutorial. Its durable content is the decision method, ownership contracts and evidence practices. Current protocols, legal requirements and products will be dated and cited to minimise shelf-life risk.

### Evidence still needed to strengthen the Apress business case

The author should add 15–20 recorded interviews with relevant architects and platform leads; qualitative evidence of recurring decision problems; independent reactions to two sample chapters; and any verifiable professional audience, speaking or training reach. These are prospective validation activities and must not be described as completed until documented.

## Part 6. Primary and secondary buyers

### Primary readers

**Enterprise architect.** Owns or assures cross-domain architecture decisions. Their purchase trigger is a mandate to review multiple agentic use cases, select shared or isolated controls, document sourcing choices, justify exceptions and present decisions to governance authorities. They need consistent terms, option criteria, decision records, accountable boundaries and exit provisions. They should be able to use the book to create an evidence-backed design recommendation rather than merely replicate a diagram.

**AI platform lead.** Owns the delivery and operation of shared technical services used by several agentic use cases. Their purchase trigger is movement from a pilot portfolio to a funded multi-tenant platform, or a production incident that exposes inconsistent controls. They need service contracts, tenant isolation, Gateway enforcement, onboarding gates, verification, observability, SRE responsibilities and FinOps. They should be able to translate an approved decision into operational entry criteria and evidence.

### Secondary readers

**Security architect.** Buys for delegated authority, non-bypassable external actions, tenant isolation, memory provenance, risk exceptions and auditability. Trigger: security review, regulated workflow approval, control failure or incident response.

**Platform engineering lead.** Buys for internal service ownership, product boundaries, developer onboarding, platform-versus-domain responsibilities and sustainable scaling. Trigger: duplication across teams, rising onboarding cost or lack of a standard support model.

**SRE manager.** Buys for availability and continuity contracts, incident command, trajectory reconstruction, model-release risk, costs of failure and retirement readiness. Trigger: production onboarding, material incident, rising support burden or service-level negotiation.

**Prerequisites:** Familiarity with enterprise software architecture, service ownership, interfaces and technical risk. No prior experience with a specific agent orchestration SDK is assumed.

**Purchase format:** Individual professional reference; team reference for architecture reviews, platform design and security assurance; supplementary material supports workshops but does not replace the book.

## Part 7. Measurable learning outcomes

After reading and applying the worked examples, a reader will be able to:

1. **Decide whether to share** agent services by completing the D-01 adoption record with funded demand, alternatives, benefit/cost evidence and deviation triggers (Chapters 1, 4, 23, 41).
2. **Appraise sourcing options** by comparing build, buy, embed and partner against control ownership, evidence export and exit (Chapters 5, 23, 50).
3. **Assign ownership** by creating a responsibility matrix separating central Fabric contracts from domain-owned use-case outcomes (Chapters 3, 6, 35).
4. **Specify tenant boundaries** by documenting identity, context, policy, audit and cost isolation and the negative tests required to verify them (Chapters 7, 21, 28).
5. **Trace a request through the canonical model** and assign each runtime responsibility to the four Structural Layers or the relevant Control Plane (Chapters 10–22).
6. **Constrain delegation** by identifying the actor chain, permitted actions, resource scopes, expiry and revocation conditions (Chapters 15–16).
7. **Evaluate a mutable action** by inspecting a policy receipt and an enforcement path, including at least one denied action and a negative bypass case (Chapter 16).
8. **Assess context provenance** by reviewing a Context Manifest and distinguishing reliable retrieval from unresolved source conflict (Chapter 13).
9. **Define governed memory controls** by specifying source, policy class, write receipt, retention and contradiction handling (Chapters 13, 21).
10. **Set an autonomy boundary** by linking permitted actions and escalation conditions to behavioural assertions and promotion evidence (Chapters 9, 18, 33).
11. **Review a release** by applying verification assertions, rejection criteria, rollback conditions and an explicit gate report (Chapters 18, 39, 47).
12. **Reconstruct an incident** from a trajectory, relevant authorisation records, tool observations and supporting evidence (Chapters 17, 37, 40, 48).
13. **Estimate cost per successful task** and explain how model, tool, infrastructure, evaluation and failed-task costs affect a use-case budget (Chapters 8, 20, 38).
14. **Apply the ten playbooks** by checking their entry criteria, required artefacts, actors, gates, abort conditions and completion evidence (Chapters 41–50).
15. **Plan retirement** by specifying access revocation, tenant transition, data handling, evidence retention, rollback/rehearsal and a signed exit record (Chapters 34, 49–50).
16. **Complete a decision review** using the 30-page Atlas format: context, default, trade-off, deviation condition, reversibility and artefact (Chapter 23).

Evidence of learning should be demonstrated through completed artefacts and scenario exercises, not an unsupported claim that readers can deploy a production-ready platform merely by reading the book.

### Chapter 1 — The Case for a Shared Fabric

Many organisations introduce agents through separate teams and products, creating repeated tool integrations and inconsistent controls before they have evidence that a common service is economically justified. This chapter distinguishes a shared Fabric from an individual use case or product and identifies the investment and risk conditions that justify considering shared infrastructure. It follows Meridian Financial as parallel pilots encounter duplicated decision rights and inconsistent proof of action. The reader finishes with a Fabric opportunity brief that records the reason to investigate a common service rather than assuming one is required; the resulting adoption choice is formalised in D-01 and tested against Chapter 4.

### Chapter 4 — When Not to Build a Fabric

A shared Fabric creates service ownership, security responsibilities, platform costs and release dependencies that may not be justified by a small or isolated portfolio. This chapter specifies the conditions under which local use-case delivery, SaaS-embedded capabilities or harder segregation should be preferred, and explains why centralisation can make a system less accountable. Meridian Financial is used to show an investment proposal being narrowed when a use case cannot provide evidence of shared-control demand. The reader completes a No-Fabric decision memo with explicit alternatives and reconsideration triggers, linking D-01 and D-02 to the sourcing analysis in Chapter 5.

### Chapter 5 — Build, Buy, Embed or Partner

Once an enterprise has established that shared or delegated capabilities are needed, it must decide which responsibilities to own and which to contract for. This chapter compares build, buy, embed and partner choices using common dimensions: enforceable controls, integration scope, evidence export, residency obligations, operational support and exit reversibility. Meridian Financial evaluates an embedded workflow against a cross-system process in which authority and auditability cannot be outsourced without contractual proof. The reader produces the sourcing option appraisal for D-02, including the trigger that would change the preferred choice and a list of required supplier evidence.

### Chapter 9 — Earned Autonomy and Sponsorship

Autonomy is not a label awarded by a model vendor; it is a set of permitted actions and operating limits approved by accountable owners. This chapter explains the Autonomy Envelope, approval and escalation boundaries, and how release and production evidence support an increase or reduction in permitted activity. Meridian Financial begins with approval-gated actions and encounters an exception that forces a narrower operating envelope rather than a general waiver. The reader creates an autonomy promotion charter specifying sponsor, thresholds, review date and withdrawal conditions; the technical verification mechanism is explained in Chapter 18 and formalised by D-05, D-16 and D-23.

### Chapter 13 — Context

Agent behaviour depends on the sources retrieved and the claims accepted as context, but a plausible answer is not evidence that its inputs were consistent or authorised. This chapter defines the Context Structural Layer and its responsibilities for retrieval, semantic identity, memory, provenance, contradiction handling and context assembly. A Meridian Financial scenario exposes two conflicting authoritative records; the system preserves both claims, records a Context Manifest and escalates a decision it cannot safely resolve. The reader can specify provenance and memory controls through D-11–D-14 and review a sample manifest, conflict record and governed memory-write decision without introducing a separate Semantic Context Control Plane.

### Chapter 16 — Gateway Control Plane

An agent may have a valid session while lacking authority for a particular external side effect. This chapter explains the Gateway Control Plane, its mutable-action enforcement contract, policy receipts and the infrastructure conditions required to prevent bypass through alternate credentials or egress paths. Meridian Financial attempts a tool action outside the caller's delegated scope; the gateway denies it and the team verifies that a direct adapter path is equally blocked. The reader learns to inspect a policy receipt, prove enforcement using negative tests and define escalation when the policy service is unavailable; the relevant frozen decisions are D-10 and D-18, with identity ownership detailed in Chapter 15.

### Chapter 23 — The Fabric Decision Atlas

Architecture discussions often end with a recommendation but omit the evidence, assumptions and conditions that would justify changing it. This chapter provides the book's 30 fixed one-page decision records, spanning adoption, ownership, isolation, runtime controls, governance, economics, verification, interoperability and retirement. Every page uses the same F1 contract: context, default, trade-off, deviation conditions, reversibility and artefact, so decisions can be compared and reviewed consistently. The reader can select a decision, complete its evidence record and follow the cross-references to the detailed mechanism or playbook without treating the default as universal policy.

### Chapter 38 — Unit Economics, Showback and Chargeback

Model-token prices alone do not express the full operating cost of an enterprise use case. This chapter explains how to attribute model, tool, compute, evaluation, retry and failure costs to tenants and use cases, and how to distinguish successful completion from raw request volume. Meridian Financial discovers that a lower apparent cost per model call has not reduced cost per successful task because recovery and repeated attempts are consuming the savings. The reader builds a showback or chargeback ledger with explicit assumptions, reconciliation requirements and budget exceptions, connecting D-26 to the FinOps Control Plane in Chapter 20 and model strategy in Chapter 8.

### Chapter 50 — P10: Retire or Replace the Fabric

Enterprise architecture is incomplete if an organisation can introduce a shared agent platform but cannot withdraw it without losing evidence or leaving authority active. This playbook sets the entry criteria, accountable owners, transition steps, rollback conditions, deletion and retention obligations, and completion evidence for retiring or replacing the shared Fabric. Meridian Financial rehearses a tenant migration, revokes credentials, validates records that must be retained and records failures that prevent closure. Readers leave with a Fabric exit plan and a retirement rehearsal record, applying D-02, D-27 and D-30 while preserving the operational distinctions established in Chapters 34 and 49.

## Part 9. Decision Atlas positioning — proposal-ready

### The decision surface is the book's primary practical product

The **Fabric Decision Atlas** contains **30 fixed one-page architecture decisions**, not thirty alternative architectures and not a general list of best practices. Its consistent format teaches the reader to record the problem, apply a defensible default, state the trade-off, identify the condition that reverses the default, classify reversibility, and produce a reviewable artefact. Where evidence is insufficient, the page requires a review or a deliberately unresolved choice rather than a confident but unsupported recommendation.

Each decision page links to the chapters that explain its underlying mechanism and to the lifecycle or playbook stage where the decision is executed. This gives the book two reading modes: a practitioner can read the complete architecture, or retrieve a single decision during a design review and follow only the required evidence trail. The Atlas therefore supports the enterprise architect's design-authority role and the platform lead's implementation responsibility without confusing their accountabilities.

### How to describe it in acquisition discussions

- **Not:** A proprietary industry standard or proof that no other author discusses similar subjects.
- **Not:** A vendor comparison grid or product recommendation.
- **Not:** Thirty guaranteed answers for every jurisdiction and operating context.
- **Yes:** A uniform decision-record method, explicitly tied to defaults, deviations, owners, artefacts and executable playbooks.
- **Yes:** A repeatable reader takeaway that can be shown in the submission samples.

**Proposed physical presentation:** One printed page per decision, with stable D-01 to D-30 identifiers, a navigable chapter cross-reference and a companion editable record where rights and production arrangements permit. The printed book remains independently usable if no companion site is available.

**Editorial proof:** Include six complete F1 pages in the Apress submission sampler. Verify that each page fits the approved format without shrinking text or removing a required field. No decision ID or default is changed by this proposal.
